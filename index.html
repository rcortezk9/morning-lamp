Daily Formation Synthesis.

What is the Church teaching your soul today?

Today the Church calls you to examine your hands — what do they do with the authority you have, however small, over someone else's access to God? Paul held real power over the Corinthian church and used it to wound only enough to heal, then dropped it the instant mercy could take over. The scribes and Pharisees held real power too, and used it to multiply distance between people and the God they claimed to serve. You do not need a pulpit to face this test: every parent, every friend giving counsel, every believer who corrects another in love or in pride is standing at this same fork. Today, choose to be a door someone can walk through, not a lock on it.

Ascetic posture for today: Before you correct or judge anyone today — in speech, in silence, or in your own heart — ask whether your aim is their restoration or your own righteousness, and let that answer decide whether you speak at all.

From the Orthodox Study Bible

2 Corinthians 2:4True pastoral correction brings both agony for the overseer—affliction, anguish of heart, many tears—and abundant love to the one being disciplined (see Heb 12:5, 6, 11).
2 Corinthians 2:5-11Church discipline must not be too severe; that is, it must not go farther than the sin. And not to forgive and comfort (v. 7) the one who repents is itself a sin. Undue separation from the Church through excessive and prolonged discipline increases tension (too much sorrow, v. 7) and even division. This pleases Satan (v. 11), the author of strife and discord; it does not please God.
2 Corinthians 2:14-17Our triumph in Christ (v. 14), taken in an ancient Roman context, suggests a magnificent picture of unwilling captive soldiers paraded before the victorious commander’s chariot while fragrant flowers are strewn along the way in celebration. The victor is Christ; the captives are the powers of darkness; His fragrance (v. 15) is the apostolic ministry, especially its preaching (v. 17). Christians are the winning forces who triumph through the hearing of the word of God (v. 17) and lead the great procession of thanksgiving leading to life (v. 16).
Matthew 23:1-39Jesus spoke His final public sermon, a grand critique of the ways of the scribes and Pharisees. Several themes include: (1) The Jewish leaders have God-given authority and teach God’s Law, but are personally ungodly and cold-hearted. Their teaching is to be honored, but they are not to be imitated (vv. 2–7). (2) God is our true Father and Teacher. A teacher or father on earth is one who leads people to God. The scribes and Pharisees do the opposite, placing themselves in God’s position (vv. 8–12). (3) In His eightfold indictment of the scribes and Pharisees (vv. 13–36), Jesus charges them with inverting God’s values and with being mean-spirited, judgmental, greedy, ambitious, absorbed in externals, and blindly self-righteous. While these charges were directed against the Jewish leaders of the day, every word applies equally to those in the Church who behave in this way.
Matthew 23:13Because the example of a leader can be so influential, leaders who do not love God can hinder others from finding Him as well. Thus, leaders are held to a higher standard (Jam 3:1).
